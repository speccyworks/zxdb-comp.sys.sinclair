             The Driver review by Simon Cooke

                  Picture the scene: Budding journalist, filled with the
             heady atmosphere of the Gloucester show, runs over to the
             Revelation stall and buys a brand new pristine copy of The
             Driver, Steve Taylor's brand new SAM product, which promised
             to do for the SAM what the cotton industry did for sandpaper
             Y-fronts (In that it makes everything seem a lot more
             tasteful, palatable, and infinitely less scratchy). I tried
             in vain to find a free machine to play around with it on,
             and unfortunately was immensely unsuccessful. Still, it was
             only a short train journey until I'd be back in my flat in
             studentville, Manchester, able to zing my mouse cursor
             around the screen in long awaited bliss.
                  There are a couple of clauses in the above sentence
             that are blatantly false (not including the crack about
             undergarments), but I'll leave you to decide what they are
             in a decidedly obtuse manner and get on with the review (oh,
             for the irrelevant, irreverant days of YS... how I miss
             them).
                  I suppose I'd better explain exactly what The Driver
             is. If you've ever seen a PC running Windows, an Apple
             Macintosh, an Atari ST or an Amiga, you've seen what a
             Graphical User Interface is (or GUI for short). They
             normally are controlled using a small hand-held device
             called a mouse, which controls an arrow-shaped pointer on
             the screen. This can be moved around to click on different
             parts of the screen to control programs, incorporating such
             things as pull-down menus, windows and buttons.
                  To fully explain what a GUI is would take a lot more
             space than is available to me for this article, so find a
             friend with one of these machines and ask them to show you
             their WIMP environment (yet another abbreviation, meaning a
             lot of pretty pictures). A GUI, when it comes down to it, is
             a very picturesque way of presenting a program, making it
             instantly accessible and incredibly easy to control.
                  And now we've got one on the SAM. Hurrah!
                  Actually, come to think about it, better not break out
             the champagne yet -- there's something important that I have
             to tell you about the system. But first, what does it look
             and feel like?
                  At first glance, it looks very Amiga-ry. The colour
             scheme, for one, is orange, white, grey and black -- this
             can be changed of course, using a utility that is supplied
             on the disc. It's quite a pretty system, with lots of nice
             Icons too. The SAM having a pretty small screen resolution
             doesn't help much, but other than that the system copes
             admirably. There is a program-identifier bar across the top
             of the screen (with a shut-down button to the right of the
             bar), and below that there is a menu-bar, on which the pull-
             down menu's sit. This is all fair enough, but I can't help
             but think that the space could have been better used by
             dropping the ID bar to give more room for the actual program
             itself. Also, having been weaned on Windows, the shut-down
             button being on the right of the screen is a little
             annoying, but that's a tiny personal niggle.
                  When the program starts up, it enters File Manager
             mode, giving a directory of the current disc (in quite a
             picturesque format too). The directory is held in a window
             on the screen, with scroll bars and movement and sizing
             buttons, and a close button (oooh! just like the GUI's on
             the "big" machines). Other programs on the master disc (as
             well as File Manager, that is) are an Icon designer, the
             aforementioned Preferences utility, a brief tutorial on the
             basics of using The Driver, a calculator program and a
             sliding puzzle game. And a wordprocessor -- which is the
             reason I bought the utility in the first place -- at last, a
             SAM wordprocessor which used ASCII rather than filling
             everything with spaces, as Outwrite and Tasword were wont to
             do.
                  Soooo.... it would appear that we have one of the SAM
             dreams come true -- excellent software at last. The only
             problem is that there are a lot of bugs and niggles with it.
                  A minor niggle is that instead of being able to click
             on name-bar of a window and drag it around the screen using
             that, to move windows you have to click on a special Move
             button. This is unlike every other system on the market, and
             totally disregards the GUI programmer's bible -- the Apple
             Human Interface guidelines.
                  More serious bugs are those that occur when the File
             Manager is sorting the directory listing prior to displaying
             it on the screen -- if you select that same drive again to
             open it up and read the directory, it can't handle it and
             proceeds to crash, giving an Abort, Continue or Reset
             option. This should have been spotted. Also, at spurious
             times the screen doesn't properly update, giving bits of
             information boxes being left piecemeal across the display
             area.
                  In order to use The Driver to access your own programs
             with an icon of your choice (an icon being a pretty picture
             representing your program), you have to create a small file
             called an "application bootstrap". This is all well and
             good, and quite acceptable, but for one tiny problem. The
             part of File Manager that creates these is slightly bugged,
             leaving part of the file-name of your program being written
             over the top of the icon, resulting in the top two lines of
             the icon looking like a mess of random colours.
                  Perhaps the most annoying bugs are those that inhabit
             the Notepad program (the wordprocessor that accompanies The
             Driver). One of these occurs during regular use -- when
             typing on a large document, the screen update is so slow as
             to lose text if you have even a slow typing speed. This
             would seem to suggest that a larger keyboard buffer is
             required, but it is an omission that should have been picked
             up during testing.
                  The other, more frightening problem occurs when working
             on larger documents, and seems to be due to cutting and
             pasting text between two copies of the Notepad. This leads
             to text being copied over and over again, and moving down
             through it just keeps putting you back at the same place,
             losing the rest of your document. It would appear (for the
             technically minded of you) to be a page-boundary problem.
                  All these bugs were found within 15 minutes extensive
             use of The Driver, which makes me wonder what Revelation are
             playing at -- and Carol Brooksbank too. She wrote a glowing
             review for Format, which didn't mention any of these
             problems. Then again, Revelation themselves couldn't
             replicate many of these problems (the only one that they
             could replicate was the icon corruption bug). It wasn't just
             on my machine either; I tested it with every possible
             combination of DOS version and hardware configuration, but
             the problems persisted whichever I used. Their inability to
             replicate problems that I gave them explicit instructions on
             how to find is worrying, as is the apparent lack of beta-
             testing.
                  Now it must be said that being an experienced technical
             writer and reviewer, I do tend to put a program more through
             its paces than most people would, but these bugs should all
             have been spotted before this program went on sale. It would
             appear to me that Revelation rushed this one out in order to
             make quick profits. More worrying is that in the reply to my
             letter, I was told that the disc was fine when compared to
             their master (which I knew -- I never said that the disc was
             faulty, I said that the program was faulty and in an
             unusable and unsaleable state). I was also told that of the
             faults that they could replicate (ie one of them), a fix for
             them would be made available on an upgrade disc, the arrival
             of which would be made public through Format and Fred
             magazine. Unfortunate really, seeing that I don't subscribe
             to either of them. Besides, I should really ask for a full
             refund, as the program is unusable (no matter what they say
             about it gaining support amongst its fans, and that it is
             still usable with the bugs as they are). It also would
             appear that this upgrade is not free either, which is
             insulting to say the least.
                  Another unforgiveable thing that Revelation have done
             is not to give technical information and pre-release copies
             to developers. It would appear to be impossible for any
             programmer other than Steve Taylor himself to write for The
             Driver at the moment, which is annoying to say the least.
             Looks like I may be writing my own GUI in the future.
             (Journalist sighs and shakes his head in despair).
                  Okay, to wrap this up, this is needed software. Send
             off for it by all means, and use it. Support it -- the
             support is needed. But if it's bugged when you get it, annoy
             the hell out of Revelation. Your statutory consumer rights
             mean that now they have been informed of the faults, they
             cannot sell the product with the faults without providing a
             free upgrade. If you're really incessed by it, send back
             your disc and demand a refund -- they have to give it to
             you.
                  If the bugs get removed, the SAM could really have a
             nice front-end at last.
                  One last note, which is quite worrying. (Motions to the
             readers to move in closer, and lowers his voice). Revelation
             have their discs duplicated by Format publications. Fair
             enough, I thought. It was then sent back to Revelation,
             apparently with nothing wrong with it. Again, fair enough.
             And then I looked at the postmark on the envelope.
                  Surprisingly, for a company based in Essex, Revelation
             software has a tendancy to go a long way to post its
             letters. Gloucester in fact. Now there are a lot of rumours
             about Revelation being run by Bob Brenchley rather than
             Frank Broughton. In fact, according to some sources, Frank
             either doesn't exist, or is a relation of Bob's. The plot
             thickens.
                  Come to think of it, thinking of some of the very mis-
             informed comments that Bob has written in his letters page
             over the past year, selling a product like The Driver,
             without beta-testing is something I wouldn't put past him.
                  But of course, that's all rumour and hearsay. Isn't it?

             Rating: 4/10, but if it gets completely debugged, I'll give
             it a 6. Not as good as it could be, but it remains to be
             seen what programmers and developers can do with it.

			Additional (not in published article)

                 ++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++

             Having seen Driver v2.0 at the show, it's well worth waiting
             for. Unfortunately, Bob Brenchley is being very sneaky about
               the whole thing and not letting anyone actually *see* it
             until it's released fully -- so that he can charge existing
             users extortionate amounts for the upgrade. V2.0 solves all
             of the problems I mention above, and is very Windows-esque -
                             - and works incredibly well.
              It also is going to be Midget compatible for all those of
                                 you who want it !!!

              Steve Taylor, it seems, is not to blame, and from a lot of
              what I heard at the show (reasons for hardware alterations
              to make the SAM Elite, etc), Bob Brenchley is just playing
                                         god.

                 +++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++

             Simon Cooke (YS's ex Tech Ed, head of Entropy)

Ah. Right. For those that don't know, I 'protect' Revelation's games (I use
'protect' in quotes to keep Simon happy...) and get paid a flat rate of 25
quid per game. Hell, it means I can live for another week...

Anyway. I get the game from Bob. He signs the letter. It has the Format header
across the top. It's got the Format address in the top-right. It's postmarked
Gloucester.

And then I send it back. To Bob. In Gloucester.

And then a few days later I get a cheque. From Bob's building society account.
In Gloucester. Signed by Bob. Although later payments are more like a few
weeks later than a few days...

And according to someone (I won't mention who) the address in Essex is Bob's
mum's address. But I don't know this for sure. But he usually gets things
right does this bloke, so I'm willing to trust his word on this one...

And that's all I'm saying ;-)

My position is as follows.

1. Although I do still have a real Spectrum, I never use it. As the
years go by, fewer and fewer people will use real Spectrums, as the
poor old things will eventually break and it won't be possible to
repair them. In fact, avoiding using your old Spectrum is proabably a
good idea for the same reason. For this reason, and because the
emulators are getting very good, there will be less and less need to
transfer TAPs for use on old machines.

2. If you use a TAP file, you usually have fiddle with the emulator
sligthly to get it to work - e.g. possibly select the tape mode, and
select whether you want the emulator to be in 48K or 128K mode. Then
you have to type the command - or press Enter! ;-) - to load the TAP.
With a snapshot, you can just load it and it starts. It's therefore
more convenient.

3. With a large number of TAPs, the loading screen flashes by so
quickly that you don't have a chance to look at it.

Now don't get me wrong. I'm all in favour of both TAP and TZX files.
However, I also like perfect snapshots: ones that display the loading
screen at the start, until you press a key, and then begin as though
you'd just loaded them.

'Perfect snaps'
---------------

In their favour:

(a) They let you look at the loading screen
(b) They're convenient, because you don't have to select emulator
    hardware
(c) They're efficient, because TAPs aren't compressed on disc and Z80
    snapshots are

Against them:

(a) They don't preserve the tape's original contents exactly (though
    it may be possible to reconstruct the TAP from the snapshot)
(b) You need two of them if the game behaves differently in 48K and
    128K modes

TAP files
---------

In their favour:

(a) They preserve tapes in their original form
(b) They often serve both 48K and 128K versions of a game in one file

Against them:

(a) They require you to fiddle with emulator settings
(b) They don't load as quickly as snapshots
(c) You don't usually get to look at the loading screen properly

TZX files of course address some of the problems of TAP files, and are
ideal for preserving tapes perfectly; but they take ages to load, and
therefore lead us back, full circle, to the perfect snapshot!

My position is that I want TAPs if making a perfect snapshot causes
anything to be lost; for example Nonterraqueous has two loading
screens, and of course a snap will only let you see one of them.
However, for 'simple' tapes, of the sort where there's a Basic loader
followed by a SCREEN$ followed by some code, I want a perfect snap
rather than a TAP.

TAPs and TZXs are great, but they don't cater ideally for all
circumstances. For me, I'd like archives such as WOS to include both
a perfect TAP or TZX and a perfect Z80-format snapshot as well. All
three formats have their place.

I do dislike the use of 'imperfect' snapshots, of course - ones that
start part-way through a tune, or have someone's name in the high-
score table or something... but they're a different case.

  My preferred format for Spectrum files is +3DOS binaries, because of the
transfer software I use (MSODBALL converts 720k +3DOS discs <--> 720k PCDOS
discs). This format (together with SNA for 48k files) allows me the best
access to the data within the files from the host computer, with as little 
fiddling as possible. The separate files give me the ability to vary the 
file sizes, which isn't very easy in a TAP. These two formats also have the
very great benefit of absolute position - given any byte in the file, it is
very easy to work out where it belongs in Spectrum memory (or vice versa). 

  JSW128, for example, is a set of +3DOS files; it is supplied with TAPCAT,
which combines them all into a .TAP file for emulators. Its PC-hosted editor
(JSWED) manipulates the +3DOS files, and can import data from a .SNA.
  The other programs I've written which manipulate Spectrum data are
UnQuill, which decompiles Quill games, and SceptEdit, which edits the 
shapes of characters' heads in Dark Sceptre. Both of these work on
.SNA files.

If the compression scheme is well defined, it should (in my opinion)
simply be a level between the harddisk and the utility.

(just like 'stdio.h' routines do). The spooling routine, which takes
care of decompressing, should then be made public.
An emulator/utility author could simply insert that code and it will
work.

makes any sense (e.g. do you see any significant time lag when
decompressing a Z80 file? If so, consider rewriting your decompression
routine.)

size. You can simply stick more software on 1 CD while emulators could
still use the software directly.

Why would anyone compress _anything_? To save storage space.
